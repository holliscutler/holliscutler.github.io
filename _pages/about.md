I'm a software engineer and researcher working on bio-inspired AI, with a
background across machine learning, robotics and automation, and geospatial
systems, shaped by work and study in the U.S., Taiwan, and abroad.

I recently finished an M.S. at the National Taipei University of Technology,
where I spent two years on a question that sounds strange until you look at it
closely: whether fungal mycelium and the slime mould *Physarum polycephalum* —
organisms with no brain and no central controller — suggest a workable design
for predicting data that arrives continuously and will not sit still. They
solve routing problems by reinforcing the paths that carry flow and letting the
rest fade, which turns out to be a serviceable description of what a forecaster
needs to do when the thing it is modelling keeps changing. That work became a
paper at ALIFE 2026 and a thesis, both covered under [research](/research/).

Before Taipei I read Computer Science and Chinese Language and Literature at the
University of Virginia. The engineering I did around it was less tidy than a
research track: indoor wayfinding across a hospital system, sidewalk
accessibility mapping in QGIS for local nonprofits, a scheduling tool for a food
pantry, and a stint at IBM reworking the message-queue logic behind Red Hat
Marketplace. Then, between the degree and graduate school, ten months on organic
farms across Ireland, Scotland, and New Zealand.

In Taipei I co-founded the **Taiwan AI Startups Community**, which brings
together founders, engineers, and researchers working on AI here — talks,
panels, workshops, and the kind of conversations that only happen in person. I
build and maintain its website.

I'm interested in systems that adapt to data rather than assume it holds still,
in the useful parts of biological computation rather than the metaphorical ones,
and in getting models to run somewhere smaller than a datacentre. I'm currently
open to engineering and research roles.
